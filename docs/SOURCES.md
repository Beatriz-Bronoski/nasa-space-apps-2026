# Still Here: research sources and evidence boundaries

Sources checked on November 14, 2026. The references below explain the problem that motivates Still Here. They do not establish that this prototype improves relationships, mental health, or mission performance, and they do not imply NASA endorsement.

## 1. NASA Human Research Roadmap

- **Title:** Human Research Roadmap (HRR).
- **Corporate author:** NASA Human Research Program (HRP).
- **Date:** Living resource; no single publication date is shown on the home page.
- **Primary source:** [NASA Human Research Roadmap](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/).

The HRR communicates NASA's Integrated Research Plan, evidence reports, external reviews, and research organization. It connects human health and performance risks to research gaps and tasks. This is the appropriate source for identifying a NASA research need; inclusion in the roadmap is not evidence that a particular product or countermeasure works.

## 2. Research gap BMed-106

- **Official title:** BMed-106: Identify effective strategies to maintain personal relations/interactions to mitigate adverse C/P/Psy/N outcomes during increasingly Earth-independent long duration missions.
- **Corporate author:** NASA HRP; responsible element: Human Factors and Behavioral Performance.
- **Page publication timestamp:** May 13, 2025, 12:40:15 PM Central, as displayed by the HRR.
- **Status when checked:** Open.
- **Primary source:** [NASA HRR gap BMed-106](https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/gaps/gap.aspx?i=705).

The gap concerns relationships with family, friends, and colleagues during exploration missions. It calls for communication support that accounts for mission constraints, assessment of relationship status, and individually tailored tools. Email, voicemail, and asynchronous video are among the communication modes proposed for evaluation. The gap's closure criteria require validated tools and countermeasures; they are research objectives rather than proof of efficacy.

**Relevance to Still Here:** An asynchronous family-connection prototype addresses a problem within this research area. Claiming that it closes BMed-106 would require evidence well beyond a working demonstration.

## 3. The 2026 family-conferencing study

**Verified journal citation:** Sheena I. Dev, Nikole D. Moody, Diana P. Arias, and Suzanne T. Bell. “Fly me to the moon: Family conferencing with communication delays on lunar missions.” _Acta Astronautica_, volume 242, May 2026, pages 48–57. [DOI: 10.1016/j.actaastro.2026.01.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actaastro.2026.01.022). [Publisher record and abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0094576526000305).

**Study and results:** Thirty-five pairs completed six weekly audiovisual private family conferences. Primary participants approximated astronaut demographics and nominated a family member. Sessions used 0, 4, 6, 8, 10, or 12 seconds of **one-way** delay. Conferences improved relationship outcomes from baseline across delay conditions. Compared with real-time sessions, relationship and well-being decrements were greater at delays of 8 seconds or more. Every tested nonzero delay reduced perceived communication quality. Participants anticipated relationship effects if such conditions continued for six months.

**Application limits:** This was a short laboratory study with astronaut-like participants, not a six-month spaceflight experiment. The six-month result describes participants' expectations. Eight seconds is an observed finding in this design, not a universal safety threshold. The study did not test Still Here, its proposed features, or delays of many minutes.

**Access note:** Direct publisher access returned HTTP 403; its indexed abstract was available. NASA records below corroborate the findings.

### NASA corroboration and recommendations

- [NASA Task Book report: Communication Delays in Cislunar Space: Behavioral Medicine Risk Characterization of Impacts on Private Family Conferences](https://taskbook.nasaprs.com/tbp/tbpdf.cfm?id=17567). Principal investigator: Sheena Dev; task progress covers August 2024–August 2025. The retrieved report was generated April 23, 2026; its bibliography cites the journal article above. Its completed-study section reports 35 pairs and six delay conditions. The earlier task-description section instead describes a planned 27-pair, five-session protocol; use the completed-study figures when describing results.
- [NASA NTRS presentation record, document 20260002496](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20260002496), published April 7, 2026. Authors: Sheena I. Dev, Nikole D. Moody, Diana P. Arias, Emma L. Lyon, Mercedes Jorge, Lauren B. Landon, Kritina L. Holden, and Suzanne T. Bell. This presentation summarizes the same study; it is not a separate replication, and its author list differs from the journal article.

The Task Book recommends pre-mission communication training and practice, and creative asynchronous options when one-way delays exceed eight seconds. These recommendations motivate design exploration; they do not establish the benefit of any particular asynchronous implementation.

## 4. Broader context and remaining uncertainty

Lauren B. Landon, John A. Karasinski, Linda G. Morissette, Katlin R. McTigue, Megan E. Parisi, Shu-chieh Wu, and Tina L. Panontin. _Assessment of the State of Communication Delay Research in Preparation for Missions Beyond Low Earth Orbit_. NASA/TM-20250003885, April 1, 2025. [NASA NTRS record and report](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20250003885).

This NASA technical memorandum reviews communication-delay research and expert interviews. It identifies evidence gaps and emphasizes that countermeasures need evaluation in the particular task and context where they will be used. Family conversation, operational coordination, and emergency support should not be assumed to have the same requirements.

For Still Here, asynchronous rituals and message features remain **design hypotheses**. Usability, perceived connection, accessibility, and effects over time require evaluation with intended users. Demonstration scenarios and simulated latency must be labeled as such; they are not operational NASA requirements or clinical evidence.
